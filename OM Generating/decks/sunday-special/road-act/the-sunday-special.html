Good morning warfighters,

My first commanding officer in the Navy, Commander Manny Hernandez, used to walk into a room and greet us the same way: “Warfighters.”

Sometimes it was serious. Sometimes it was sarcastic. Usually it was a little of both. But underneath it was a reminder that regardless of our individual roles, we were all responsible for the mission and for the people beside us.

Manny was the first leader I really looked up to in the Navy and had a tremendous impact on me as a 22-year-old officer fresh out of school. He has since passed away, but that phrase and the lesson behind it have stuck with me.

So, warfighters, welcome to my first Sunday Special.

Ben kicked this off last week, and for my first turn I wanted to start with something that has been all over the news lately and that I happen to know pretty well.

01 The Brief

USS Abraham Lincoln

The USS Abraham Lincoln has received a lot of attention recently around the conditions sailors are facing while deployed.

This one hits close to home for me.

I served aboard Lincoln from 2019 to 2021 as a Nuclear Surface Warfare Officer and Reactor Mechanical Division Officer, leading sailors responsible for operating and maintaining one of the ship’s reactor plants. During that tour, we completed a roughly 11-month deployment to the Arabian Gulf and North Arabian Sea.

I’m not aboard Lincoln today, so I’m not going to pretend to know every detail of what is happening or tell you which report is right or wrong. Legitimate concerns around food, sanitation, exhaustion and mental health should always be taken seriously.

But the coverage reminded me of something I learned very quickly in the Navy:

Things are going to go wrong.

Equipment breaks. Supplies arrive late. People get tired. Plans change. Conditions deteriorate. And when you are operating a 100,000-ton nuclear-powered aircraft carrier thousands of miles from home, the mission does not stop while everyone waits for perfect conditions.

Someone has to solve the problem.

That was a huge part of our job as officers and leaders. We were not there because we believed nothing would ever go wrong. Quite the opposite.

We raised our hands knowing that problems would come and that it would be our responsibility to deal with them when they did.

On Lincoln, that meant understanding what was happening, identifying the root cause, getting the right people involved, prioritizing resources and making decisions under pressure.

Sometimes the fix was straightforward. Sometimes it took hours or days. Sometimes you had incomplete information and still had to make the best decision you could.

The mission kept moving either way.

I think about our responsibility at Comfort in much the same way.

Real estate has problems

Water lines break. Residents move out unexpectedly. Collections get harder. Insurance premiums jump. Contractors miss deadlines. Equipment fails. Property taxes increase. You get the point.

None of those things should surprise a good operator.

Our job isn’t to promise investors that property problems will never happen. Our job is to make sure property problems don’t become investor problems.

That distinction matters to me.

When you invest with Comfort, you are trusting us with capital that took you years — sometimes decades — to build. Part of what you are hiring us to do is deal with the messiness of owning and operating real estate so you don’t have to.

You don’t want another job.

You shouldn’t have to manage the contractor who didn’t show up, figure out why collections softened or deal with a resident issue on a Saturday night.

That’s what we signed up for.

Our team has to identify problems early, make decisions, allocate resources, hold people accountable, protect the asset, preserve cash flow and keep the business plan moving forward.

And manufactured housing adds another layer of responsibility.

These aren’t just properties we own. They are communities where thousands of people live. Residents trust us with the place they call home, and investors trust us with capital they worked hard to create.

We take both responsibilities seriously.

On a ship, someone is always standing the watch. Most of the crew doesn’t need to know about every mechanical issue, every supply-chain problem or every decision happening below deck. They need to know the right people are there handling it so the larger mission can continue.

I think our investors should be able to expect something similar from us.

You should know what matters. You should hear from us when something materially changes. You should understand how your investment is performing.

But you shouldn’t have to worry about the broken water line at 2:00 AM.

That’s our problem.

And I think this becomes even more important when conditions get difficult.

When the economy is strong and everything is going according to plan, operating real estate can look pretty easy. It’s when occupancy softens, expenses increase, financing gets tighter or something unexpected breaks that you find out whether there is a real operating company behind the investment.

Those are the moments when good operations matter most.

The sailors aboard Lincoln today are dealing with a much more serious version of that reality. They are far from home, tired, operating in difficult conditions and still doing the job they volunteered to do.

They deserve our support.

And if there is one lesson from my time aboard Lincoln that has followed me into business, it is this:

You don’t raise your hand because you expect the mission to be easy. You raise your hand because you’re willing to take responsibility when it isn’t.

That is a responsibility I took seriously in the Navy, and it is one I take seriously today at Comfort.

02 Policy

21st Century ROAD to Housing Act

On July 11, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law. It’s one of the most significant pieces of federal housing legislation in years for our corner of the market, with several provisions that directly touch manufactured housing.

Let’s walk through them and what I think they could mean for our business and investors.

Cheaper Homes

The law removes the decades-old requirement that every manufactured home be built on a permanent steel chassis. That can save an estimated $5,000 to $10,000 per home and allow for more design flexibility.

My take

This is probably the cleanest near-term win in the bill. Lower factory costs should flow directly into new home orders and make it cheaper for operators like us to fill vacant lots.

Better Financing

The law also raises FHA manufactured-housing loan limits and addresses one of the biggest structural challenges in our business, financing a relatively inexpensive home.

Today, most residents in land-lease communities use chattel loans. They own the home but lease the land, so the home is treated as personal property. That usually means higher rates and shorter terms than a traditional mortgage.

The bigger opportunity is getting more manufactured homes access to mortgage-like financing. Homes can qualify as real property when they are permanently affixed to land the homeowner also owns. That opens the door to longer terms and more traditional mortgage pricing.

The ROAD Act does not change that structure for land-lease communities like ours. Our residents still own the home and lease the lot, so chattel financing remains the primary option.

Still, higher FHA limits and a broader federal push toward better MH financing are meaningful steps in the right direction.

Here is why that matters on a $70,000 home:

That is roughly $225 a month of added household capacity on the same $70,000 home. Lower financing costs make ownership more attainable, expand the buyer pool, and give residents more breathing room each month.

My take

This may be one of the highest leverage provisions in the bill for manufactured housing, but it is important to be precise about who it helps. Because our residents own their homes but lease the land, those homes remain personal property. The ROAD Act does not immediately unlock conventional mortgage financing for our tenants.

The bigger opportunity is market wide. Cheaper homes, higher FHA loan limits, and better financing can expand demand for manufactured housing overall. That includes developers building new communities and buyers placing homes on land they own. More volume through the factories should help lower costs and further normalize manufactured housing as mainstream affordable housing. That is good for the entire ecosystem, including the communities we own.

Homes are not for Corporations

The bill also includes a provision restricting institutional investors that control 350 or more single-family homes from buying additional homes.

Importantly, manufactured homes are expressly excluded, and there is no comparable restriction on manufactured housing communities.

That is notable. As housing policy becomes more interventionist, Washington appears to be treating manufactured housing more as part of the affordability solution than part of the problem.
