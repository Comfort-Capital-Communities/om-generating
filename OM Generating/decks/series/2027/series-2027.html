Market Opportunity

A new buying window is emerging

Quality communities can come to market because of ownership transitions, financing pressure and institutional portfolio needs — not because the underlying assets are impaired.

Generational Ownership Transition
Many communities remain in long-tenured private or family ownership. As founders step back and families weigh succession or estate planning, the next generation may prefer liquidity to operating responsibility — creating opportunities for experienced, long-term owners to become the community’s next steward.
Refinancing & Capital Pressure
Loan maturities and materially higher borrowing costs can make a sale more attractive than a recapitalization — even when the underlying community is performing well.
Institutional Realization & Reallocation
Closed-end funds and large allocators periodically need to monetize assets, return capital and rebalance portfolios — bringing high-quality communities to market for reasons unrelated to asset performance.
The opportunity is not limited to distressed real estate. It is quality real estate with a reason to trade.

Comfort’s operating platform, capital relationships, broker credibility, patient private capital and certainty of close position us to compete when these opportunities surface.

Tax benefits

Why Investors Love Depreciation

Depreciation is a tax deduction that recognizes how physical assets lose value over time. In real estate it lets investors offset income by writing off part of the property’s value each year, even as the property itself may rise in market value.

In manufactured-housing communities, land improvements such as roads, water lines and sewer systems depreciate over 15 years instead of the standard 27.5, capturing larger deductions earlier. Bonus depreciation writes off up to 100% of eligible improvements in year one, and even passive investors receive their share on a K-1.

Leverage amplifies the benefit. Depreciation is calculated on the community’s full cost — not just the equity invested — so financing part of the purchase with debt pairs every investor dollar with additional depreciable basis, producing a larger year-one paper loss relative to the capital an investor actually contributes.
