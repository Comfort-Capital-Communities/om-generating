What you earn vs. what you keep.

Why the cash flow you receive and the taxable income you report are not always the same number.

A few weeks ago, I wrote about one of the biggest tax benefits of real estate: depreciation. That was the first layer. I tend to think about the tax benefits of real estate in three stages:

The Three-Stage Tax Story

When you invest — depreciation.
While you own — tax-efficient cash flow.
When you sell — the 1031 exchange.

That is the same framework we use when explaining the tax story to our investors.

Today, I want to focus on the second layer — the cash flow you receive while you own the investment.

01The Playbook
The Cash Flow You Receive While You Own

Here is a quick question. Would you rather earn 6% or 4.5%? Seems obvious. But what if the 6% was fully taxable, while the 4.5% came to you without a current federal or state income tax bill?

At a 30% combined tax rate, that 6% leaves you with roughly 4.2%. The 4.5% leaves you with 4.5%. Suddenly, the answer isn't so obvious.

Here is the easiest way I know to explain it:

That's the point. At a 30% combined tax rate, a 4.5% cash-on-cash return that isn't currently taxed leaves you with more cash than a fully taxable 6% return. At a 40% tax rate, that same 4.5% is equivalent to earning about 7.5% on a fully taxable investment.

What you earn matters. What you keep matters more.
02How It Works
So How Can Real Estate Do That?

This is one of the more interesting parts of owning real estate:

The cash hitting your bank account and the taxable income showing up on your K-1 can be two very different numbers.

Part of that comes from depreciation, which we covered in the last tax piece. Real estate can produce actual cash flow while depreciation reduces the taxable income reported to an investor. But there is another concept worth understanding: return of capital.

When investors first hear that phrase, there is an obvious question: "Wait — are you just giving me my own money back?"

No. The property is still producing real cash from operations. For tax purposes, certain partnership distributions can reduce your tax basis rather than creating an equal amount of current taxable income.

I think about it much more simply:

The property produced the cash. The tax code determines when you pay tax on it.

That timing matters. Money you don't have to send out in taxes today can stay in your bank account, be reinvested, or continue working somewhere else.

One Important Distinction

"Return of capital" is a tax concept. It does not mean your monthly distributions are simply paying down your original investment. Under our investment waterfall, when there is a refinance or sale, investors receive 100% of their invested capital back before Comfort participates in the promote. The cash flow you received throughout the hold does not simply count down that original investment.

Similar language. Very different concepts.

And for most investors, that is really all you need to understand.

03The Next Layer
There Is Still One More Layer

We have now covered two parts of the tax story. Depreciation when you invest. Tax-efficient cash flow while you own. But eventually, you sell. And that is important, because some of the taxes you deferred along the way can become relevant when the investment exits.

So what happens then? That brings us to the third layer — the 1031 exchange: how you can sell a real estate investment, keep more of your capital working, and potentially continue deferring the tax bill.

The one idea I want to leave you with.

Don't just chase the highest headline return. A slightly lower cash-on-cash return that comes to you tax-efficiently can leave you with more real, spendable cash than a bigger number that gets taxed away.
